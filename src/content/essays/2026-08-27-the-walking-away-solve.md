# The Walking-Away Solve

*By Robert Chuvala*

I've done this my whole life. I just watched myself do it again and finally caught the shape.

The move looks like quitting. You're stuck on a thing, you've been stuck for a while, and instead of pushing harder you turn your back on it and start building the thing that would replace it. From the outside it reads as giving up. From the inside it's the only door that opens.

This week it was the harness. I run my whole working setup on an inherited one — someone else's hooks, someone else's assumptions baked into every layer, and I couldn't get them out by arguing with them one at a time. So I said the thing you say when you've had enough. *I'm getting rid of you.* Then I turned around and started designing its replacement. And in the act of specifying the new one — what should a good guard actually do — I solved the old one. Not as a side effect. As the whole thing. The answer to "what should any harness do" turned out to be the answer to "what's wrong with this one." I'd stared at the broken thing for weeks and couldn't see the fix. I found it the moment I stopped looking at it.

That's not new. That's the most repeated move of my life, and I have the receipts.

## The spine

I walked away from drinking on July 28, 2020, and didn't white-knuckle the old life smaller. I built a different one around the hole. Six years now. It's the lynchpin under everything else on this list.

I got fired on a Friday at 5:26 PM in February 2025. Three weeks later I asked a chatbot, *"If I don't want to go into debt and back to college to become a therapist, what other options are there for me if I want to help people?"* That question assumed the twenty years behind me were the wrong material. They weren't. The firing didn't reset the clock — it pried the same skill loose from a chair that only knew how to rent it by the hour, and I started building on my own ground instead of someone else's network.

I spent a year leaving the closed tool over and over. Every time an AI lost the thread, I didn't wait for a better model — I built the thing that held the state myself. A continuity file, then a portable context file, then a whole local system, then a fleet of them. Somewhere in there I wrote the line that was really about all of it: *"ChatGPT was the tool inside the process, not the container for the whole process."* Structure first, AI later. The container had to be mine.

I dropped the idea of one big canonical agent holding everything and built a fleet of small ones sharing a substrate instead. The sacred single timeline was the trap. State lives in the ground, not in the agent standing on it.

Same shape every time. Take the thing that was made someone else's, get it off the closed system, keep it real and yours. My old working title for the book I'm writing was *Burn It Down, Grow It Back.* I named the pattern before I understood it was a pattern.

## The two moves inside it

There are two real things stacked in the walk-away, and both do work.

The first is that you can't see a system from inside it. Embedded in the thing, every fix argues with an assumption you can't step outside of — the assumptions are the water. Walking away isn't quitting; it's the only way to get a vantage point where you can see the thing whole. Hofstadter has a name for it: *jootsing*, jumping out of the system. Some problems are invisible from the inside. You don't outthink them. You leave.

The second does the actual work. When I asked "what should any harness do," I stopped solving my one broken harness and started solving the general case — and the general case was *easier*, because generality strips the incidental. All the particular junk of this-harness fell away and I was left holding the essential requirement. And a requirement doesn't belong to any one frame, so the answer to the general case was the answer to the specific one, for free. Pólya called this the inventor's paradox: the more general problem is often easier than the special case, because it forces you down to the essence. The fix wasn't *in* the broken harness. It was one level up, in the requirement it was failing — and you only reach that level by building toward what would replace it.

## But I have to tell you about the graveyard

Because if I stopped there this would be a lie, and the whole point of the thing I built this week is that a claim doesn't get to stand just because it flatters the person making it.

Two weeks ago I said this out loud: *"it's an opioid — look at all the dead projects you eventually find and wonder how the fuck did I walk away from this thing. It's practically finished."*

Same motion. Walk away, build new. But this version has a body count. There's a folder of things at ninety percent that I have no memory of leaving, because there was no decision to leave — the attention got pulled, not withdrawn. The rush of *a new finding and direction* is the drug. The reward lands at the insight, which is immediate and free, and shipping is slow and effortful and mostly invisible, so the wiring actively trains me to abandon. I picked the word opioid on purpose. I've been sober six years. I know that mechanism from the inside.

So walking away is the most generative thing I do and the most destructive thing I do. Same gesture. I needed to know what tells them apart, because "walk away and rebuild" as blanket advice would eventually cost me everything I've half-built.

## What tells them apart

Here's the test, and I didn't invent it either — it was sitting in my own notes under Engelbart's name.

Engelbart's bootstrapping: use the system to improve the system, cumulatively, each version building on the last instead of replacing it. The healthy walk-away is bootstrapping. The new thing *subsumes* the old — it carries the old problem inside it and solves it. The harness I walked away from this week isn't in the graveyard. The replacement governs it now; the thing I left got absorbed and answered. That's the tell. When I turned around, the old problem came with me and got solved on the way.

The graveyard is the other thing. Restart from scratch, and the ninety-percent-done thing you left doesn't get subsumed, it just rots. The motion is identical from the outside. The difference is whether the new work carries the old one forward or abandons it for the hit of starting clean.

So the real technique isn't "walk away." It's: walk away *from the closed system*, toward your own ground, and only when the new thing will carry the old problem inside it. Leave the container that isn't yours. Don't leave the work that's almost done. One is jootsing. The other is the drug wearing the same coat.

I've been running the good version my whole life without a name for it, and the bad version too. A move you can only do on instinct, you can't aim — and you can't catch it turning on you. A move you can name, you can point at the next stuck thing on purpose, and you can feel the difference the moment the pull is the drug and not the door.

I told a friend this week I was walking away from something. Then I walked toward its replacement and solved the thing I'd been stuck on for a month. That's the good version. I know it's the good version because when I turned around, the old problem was still in my hands, solved, instead of behind me, rotting.
